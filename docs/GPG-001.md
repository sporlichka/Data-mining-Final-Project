## Problem Statement

The gender wage gap — the difference in average earnings between men and women — is
one of the most studied and debated issues in the US (and global) labor market.
Despite anti-discrimination legislation, women on average earn noticeably less than
men even after accounting for experience, education, and employment status.
Quantifying this gap and decomposing it into an "explained" part (driven by
observable characteristics such as education, experience, industry, occupation,
hours worked) and an "unexplained" part matters for:

- economic and social policy (anti-discrimination law, equal pay acts);
- corporate HR practices (pay transparency, compensation grading);
- understanding whether the gap is narrowing or stagnant over time.

## Project Objectives

1. Build a model that predicts a worker's wage from demographic, educational, and
   occupational characteristics.
2. Quantify the raw gender wage gap and estimate how much of it is explained by
   other features vs. attributable to sex itself.
3. Identify which factors (education, occupation, industry, experience, hours
   worked, region) have the strongest effect on wage level and on the size of the
   gender gap.
4. Track how the gap evolves over time (1981–2013).

## Research Questions / Hypotheses

| ID | Question / Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| **RQ1** | How large is the raw gender wage gap in the CPS data, and has it narrowed over time (1981 → 2013)? |
| **RQ2** | What share of the gender wage gap is explained by observable features (education, experience, hours worked, occupation, industry, region), and what share remains unexplained after controlling for them? |
| **RQ3** (hypothesis) | Sex — encoded either as `sex` (1 = male, 2 = female) or as the `female` dummy (0 = male, 1 = female) — remains a statistically and practically significant wage predictor even after education, experience, industry, and occupation are included in the model. |
| **RQ4** | Which feature groups (education, occupation/industry, experience, hours worked) have the highest predictive power for wage, and does feature importance differ between men and women? |

## Why This Dataset

- Real **Current Population Survey (CPS)** microdata — the primary US labor-market
  statistics source, used both on Kaggle and in professional labor economics
  research on the gender wage gap (e.g., Blau & Kahn).
- Already contains variables directly relevant to the topic: `sex`, `female`,
  `incwage` (annual wage income), `hrwage` / `realhrwage` (hourly, incl.
  inflation-adjusted), `potexp` / `potexp2` (potential experience), `educ99`
  (education), ~40 binary occupation/industry dummies, and region dummies
  (`northeast`, `south`, `west`, …).
- Size and scope satisfy the course requirements: **344,287 records and 234
  features** (requirement: ≥40,000 records, ≥15 features), spanning **1981–2013**,
  which also enables a time-trend analysis.
- The task maps naturally onto a **regression** problem (wage prediction), matching
  assignment Part 2.A, with room for feature-importance analysis and model
  interpretation afterward.
